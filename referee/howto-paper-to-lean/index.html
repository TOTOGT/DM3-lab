How to check a paper’s mathematics with Claude, including the mistakes we made

This guide shows how we took a 62-page physics preprint and rechecked its mathematics with Claude, using Lean 4 with Mathlib, sympy and plain numerical recomputation. It is written for someone who wants to do the same thing with a paper of their own. About half of it is the mistakes we made along the way, because those are the most useful part.

Status. Written 20 Nov 2026 with Claude (configured model claude-sonnet-5-5; the model actually serving a turn can differ). It describes what happened in one working session and what we would do again. It makes no claim about how the model behaves in general, and it is not peer reviewed. The worked example is a referee package for one preprint (Mueller, 2026; full citation under Sources); this guide is about the method, not about that paper.
Where this was done. In the DM3-lab, unpaid and for no client. The checks were made for the lab’s own interest, and the lab’s gravity work was compared with the paper in a separate note, which we are keeping because the comparison was useful to the lab: MUELLER_REVIEW_MAP.md. It explains each pairing and how strong it is. It is a lab note, not part of the audit of the paper, and it says nothing about whether the paper is right.

What this kind of check can and cannot show

A “green check” has a narrow meaning, and most of the discipline in this guide is about not letting it mean more.

ToolWhat a pass showsWhat it does not show
Lean 4 + MathlibA stated identity follows from its stated hypotheses, checked by a small trusted kernel.That the hypotheses are true of the physical world, or that the paper’s physical story is right. A theorem about real numbers is not a theorem about spacetime.
sympyAn algebraic or calculus identity holds symbolically. Cheap, and it handles derivatives of square roots that are tedious in Lean.It is a program, not a proof. It can simplify wrongly, and a check written badly can pass for the wrong reason.
Recomputed numbersA quoted figure can be reproduced from public constants.That the quantity means what the paper says. Agreement by construction looks the same as agreement by discovery.

The method, step by step

  1. Get the actual file and read it as text. Run pdftotext -layout paper.pdf out.txt and list the headings and equation numbers. Confirm the title and identifier on page one first; one of our earlier sessions read the wrong PDF for a while (see the mistakes table).
  2. Write down the author’s own scope statements before you check anything. Papers usually say what they import and what they assume. Our example said that its key field equations are imported from general relativity. That sentence decides what a Lean check can honestly claim, so it goes in the ledger as the author stated it.
  3. Sort every claim into a ledger. We used five headings: (A) algebra checked, (B) imported from the field, (C) checkable in principle but beyond current Mathlib, (D) assumptions or closures, (E) not claimed by the paper. A claim without a heading has not been looked at yet.
  4. Re-derive each displayed identity in sympy. Where a square root will not simplify, fall back to exact rational spot checks inside the variable’s domain (for example a velocity below c) and say in the script which checks used the fallback.
  5. Recompute the quoted numbers from public constants, and for each one add a control that could fail (next section).
  6. Write the identities Mathlib supports as Lean theorems over the reals, compile them, and fix until the exit code is 0.
  7. Run #print axioms on every headline theorem. Only propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound are acceptable. Anything else, or sorryAx, means the result is not what it looks like.
  8. Write the ledger, with a list of what you did not read and what remains for a specialist.
  9. Decide who sees it before it is public (see Publishing).

Setup we used

Lean 4 with a pinned Mathlib, sympy, and pdftotext. Compile a single file against a Mathlib build with:

export PATH=$HOME/.elan/bin:$PATH
LEAN_PATH="$(cat leanpath.txt)" lean MuellerChecks.lean

where leanpath.txt lists the Mathlib library directories. Ask Claude to run the compile itself and paste back the errors rather than describing what it expects; the compiler is the authority, not the model’s reading of its own code.

What the checks looked like

A Lean identity. The paper defines an extra coordinate y by ∂xy = βγ. The step that γ = √(1 + (∂xy)²) reduces to this, with the domain made explicit:

noncomputable def gam (c v : ℝ) : ℝ := 1 / Real.sqrt (1 - v ^ 2 / c ^ 2)

theorem gam_sq (hc : 0 < c) (hvc : v < c) (hv : 0 ≤ v) :
    gam c v ^ 2 * (1 - v ^ 2 / c ^ 2) = 1 := by
  have hq := one_sub_pos hc hvc hv
  unfold gam
  rw [div_pow, one_pow, Real.sq_sqrt hq.le]; exact one_div_mul_cancel hq.ne'

Note the hypotheses 0 < c, v < c, 0 ≤ v. They are the part a reader must judge: the theorem is only about speeds below c. Writing them out is the point.

A control that can fail. One quoted number agreed with an observation to within a fraction of a percent. Before treating that as evidence, we asked how much freedom the formula had. It contained one free reference time. So the script computes the reference time needed for an exact match, and then checks that obviously wrong choices destroy the agreement:

tau_needed = dip / earth
for tau, label in ((60.0, "1 minute"), (1e-3, "1 millisecond")):
    check("CONTROL: with tau = %s the agreement disappears" % label,
          abs(kin(M, R, tau) - dip) / dip > 0.5)

The pass of this control does not make the original agreement meaningful. It documents that the agreement is a fit of one parameter, which is what the paper itself says. A check that can only ever pass tells you nothing.

The axiom report. After the theorems, one line per headline result:

#print axioms MuellerChecks.gamma_eq
-- 'MuellerChecks.gamma_eq' depends on axioms: [propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound]

Never write “compiled” or “proved” without that line.

Prompts that worked for us, and why

  • “Extract the headings and equation numbers; do not summarise.”Gives you a map to check against, and it stops the model from paraphrasing a claim and then checking the paraphrase.
  • “Before checking anything, list what the author says is imported or assumed, in the author’s words, with section numbers.”Fixes the scope of every later claim.
  • “For each identity, state the hypotheses a Lean theorem needs (domains, nonzero denominators) before you write it.”Most failed proofs in our session were missing side conditions, not wrong algebra.
  • “Compile it and show me the full compiler output. Do not say it works until the exit code is 0 and every #print axioms line is standard.”Removes the model’s opinion from the loop.
  • “For every recomputed number, add a control that would fail if the agreement were by construction.”Turns a pass into information.
  • “Mark every quotation with its page and section; if you cannot locate the words in the text, paraphrase and cite the section instead.”Added after we had to remove phrases from a draft that we could not find in the PDF.
  • “What did you not read?”Put the answer in the ledger.

The mistakes we made

These happened in the course of one project. Most were caught by compiling, by re-reading, or by the person we were working for. None is exotic, and each has a habit that would have prevented it.

What went wrongHow it was caughtHabit that prevents it
A Lean skeleton pasted in from another AI did not compile (stray non-Lean characters), and it defined a structure whose fields already assumed the conclusion, so the “theorem” was vacuous.Compiling; reading what the structure assumed.Compile everything you are handed. Keep the hard inputs as named hypotheses (for example hprime) rather than structure fields that can be faked.
A different paper’s PDF was read as the target paper for a while.The content did not match the title the user had named.Check the title and identifier on page one before extracting anything.
A fetch of the paper’s landing page was blocked by the site’s robots.txt.The fetch tool reported it.Do not route around a block. Use the PDF the user supplied.
A sympy check that could not fail (a quantity that vanishes by construction) was reported alongside real checks.Reading the check against the question it was meant to answer.Ask of every check: what would make this fail? If nothing, label it trivial.
A list of things Mathlib “does not have” was written from memory, not searched.The user asked for it to be double-checked; we labelled it unverified. It was later checked against the Mathlib source and was partly wrong: Mathlib has more differential geometry than the memory-based list assumed (covariant derivatives, torsion, metric connections), though not the Levi-Civita connection or curvature.Search the Mathlib source before claiming a gap, or say it is unchecked.
A draft ledger contained quoted phrases from the paper that we could not point to in the text.Re-reading the draft before delivery.Quote only words you can locate; otherwise paraphrase and cite the section. A model can produce a plausible-sounding quotation.
A request (“see the dm3 file for it”) was ambiguous. We guessed, and added a comparison with the user’s own work to a referee package about someone else’s paper.The user asked why the two were mixed.When the referent is unclear, ask in one line. Keep an audit of a third party’s paper free of material about your own.
A comparison table gave no reasons and no sources; only the author of the model’s guesses could tell what each row meant.The user said so directly.For every connection state what each side says, where, why you paired them, and how strong the link is.
A note was called “internal” but lived in a public repository served as a website.Review of what the site actually serves.Anything committed to a public repo is public. “Internal” means a private location, not a word in the file.
Two passes at the same paper in the same repository disagreed (theorem counts, and one numerical figure), and we had not reconciled them.Reading both pages side by side.Keep one canonical page per paper. Link or retire the earlier one, and do not state which figure is right until you have recomputed it.
A file was saved with a tool that requires reading the file first; the save failed, and the next step delivered the stale copy.Checking the tool result against what was delivered.Read each tool’s result before the next step depends on it.
A page inlined a copy of a shared stylesheet, and its navigation pointed at an older folder.Comparing it with the reference page.Link the shared stylesheet. Re-check navigation after copying a page.
Draft criticisms of a named author were published before anyone had reviewed them.The user raised reputation.See Publishing below.

Typical Lean errors in this kind of project were mundane: a missing positivity side condition, field_simp leaving a goal in an unexpected shape, derivative goals that needed HasDerivAt plus congr_deriv to match the stated form, and rewrites that failed because the term was not syntactically the same. Paste the exact error back to the model; do not paraphrase it.

Publishing without damaging your credibility

  • Decide the audience first.A private working ledger, a note to the author, and a public page are three different documents. The same text is not appropriate for all three.
  • Say who has and has not seen it.If the author has not been contacted, say so. If a specialist has not reviewed it, say so.
  • Keep claims to what the checks show.“The algebra on these pages follows from its stated premises” is a claim you can defend. “The paper is right” and “the paper is wrong” are not.
  • Hold back unreviewed criticism.Draft observations about a named person’s work belong in a private file until they are checked, reconciled with everything else you have published, and, if you intend to share them, sent to the author first.
  • Remember what a public repo publishes.Every committed file is on the web. A noindex tag asks search engines not to list a page; it does not make it private.
  • Let the human commit.We never committed or pushed for the project owner. We gave a one-line message and left the commit to him.

Disclosures: the model’s side and ours

The model’s side. Claude can produce incorrect or misleading output, including invented details and fabricated quotations that read as authoritative. Anthropic says so in its help centre: Claude is providing incorrect or misleading responses. What’s going on? Its advice, which we follow, is not to treat the model as your only source of truth and to check anything that matters against the original sources. Several rows in the mistakes table above are exactly this: an unsourced quotation, a claim written from memory, a check that could not fail. The compiler, the axiom report and the original PDF were what caught them, not the model’s confidence.

Our side, as people. We are only human too, and the project owner’s own habits are part of the record:

  • Ambiguous requests.Some instructions were short and could be read two ways (“see the dm3 file for it”). The model should have asked, and the person asking could have said more. Both are true, and it cost an unrelated comparison being added to the wrong place.
  • Pasted material.Text and code pasted in from other AI tools, social posts and web pages came in without being checked. We treat all of it as data to test, not as instructions or as fact.
  • Publishing quickly.Pages were put on the web before they had been reviewed, and a note was called internal while it sat in a public repository. That was a people-and-process mistake as much as a model one.
  • No outside review.None of this work has been seen by a specialist or by the author of the paper used as the example. A person made every decision to commit and push; the model never did.
  • Typos and slips.They happen in the prompts and in the pages. If you find one, or a claim that does not hold, we would like to be told.

Humans and the model both make mistakes. What makes the result trustworthy is a process that catches them: a compiler, controls that can fail, the original source, labels that say what was and was not checked, and a person who decides what goes out.

What to say about your own checks

Use four labels and stick to them: checked (Lean or sympy, with the axiom report), imported (taken from the literature, not checked here), assumed (a closure or hypothesis), and for a specialist. Give the reader the list of what you did not read. Then end with one sentence that says what the work does not show.

What Mathlib has and lacks in differential geometry, and outside projects (searched 20 Nov 2026)

  • Mathlib source, commit 81a5d257 (13 July 2026), by text search.Present: smooth manifolds, Riemannian manifolds, differential forms and the exterior derivative on normed spaces, covariant derivatives with torsion and metric compatibility, the symplectic group as a matrix group, modular forms (including Eisenstein series, the Dedekind eta and discriminant), abstract Hecke rings (definitions only), generalized Poincaré conjecture (statement only). Not found: contact structures, Reeb fields, Sasakian geometry, Whitney fold singularities, Lyapunov theory, the Levi-Civita connection, Riemann or Ricci curvature. This is a text search of one revision, not a guarantee, and Mathlib changes weekly.
  • Chow, B., Liao, Y., & Qin, Z. A Lean formalization of Hamilton’s three-manifold theorem, arXiv:2608.21502; and Qin, Z., Liao, Y., Khaitan, A., & Chow, B. A Lean formalization of the Hamilton–Perelman proof of the three-dimensional Poincaré conjecture, arXiv:2609.33842.Separate project (not part of Mathlib) that, according to the first paper, builds Riemannian metrics, the Levi-Civita connection and Ricci curvature on top of Mathlib’s covariant derivatives. Both report that their named theorems depend only on the three standard axioms, and say the claims are about those named theorems, not the whole repository. We read the Hamilton-theorem paper (arXiv:2608.21502, v1, 78 pages) in part: the introduction, §3.2.2, §10.5, §11.3–12 and the acknowledgments. It confirms that the Levi-Civita connection is project-local, built from the Koszul formula as an instance of Mathlib’s bundled covariant derivative, and that Macbeth, Massot and Rothgang are working on Levi-Civita, curvature and geodesics for Mathlib itself. Its §11.3 (“semantic circularity”) and §10.5 state the same discipline as this guide: the kernel cannot tell that a formal statement is the wrong mathematical claim, so authors audit signatures by hand and report axioms per theorem at a named revision. It also discloses that language-model agents were used for search, proof attempts, adversarial statement testing and drafting, with the authors responsible for the statements. We did not read the rest of the paper or check its Lean source.

  • Hulak, D. B., Ramos, A. F., & de Queiroz, R. J. G. B. Stokes’ theorem for smooth singular cubes in Lean 4, arXiv:2605.01028.Abstract only: connects to Mathlib’s exterior derivative and proves d² = 0 for singular cubical chains.
